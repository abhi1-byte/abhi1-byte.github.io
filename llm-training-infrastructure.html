Infrastructure & Training

A visual breakdown of the key steps involved in training large language models and the real resource costs associated with building systems like GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA.

Training Pipeline From raw data to deployed model
1
Data Collection & Curation
Assembling the Training Corpus
Billions of text tokens are gathered from web crawls (Common Crawl), digitized books, Wikipedia, code repositories, and licensed content. Raw data is filtered for quality, deduplicated, and tagged by language and domain. The corpus must be large enough and diverse enough to expose the model to the full range of language patterns it will encounter in deployment.
Common CrawlWebTextBooksWikipediaGitHub codeQuality filtering
2
Tokenization & Preprocessing
Converting Text to Numbers
Text is split into tokens using a byte-pair encoding (BPE) tokenizer. Each token is mapped to a numerical ID. The tokenized corpus is formatted into fixed-length sequences and stored in a format optimized for parallel processing across thousands of GPU cores. GPT-4 uses a tokenizer with approximately 100,000 token types.
BPE tokenizationSequence packingShard formatting~100K vocabulary
3
Pre-Training
Learning Language from Scale
The model is trained on the full corpus using next-token prediction: given a sequence of tokens, predict the next one. This objective is run across trillions of examples over weeks or months using thousands of GPUs or TPUs in parallel. The model learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and world knowledge purely from predicting text. This is the most computationally expensive phase.
Next-token predictionTransformer architectureDistributed trainingTrillions of tokens
4
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
Teaching the Model to Follow Instructions
Human contractors write example conversations demonstrating the desired assistant behavior: answering questions clearly, refusing harmful requests, and maintaining a helpful tone. The pre-trained model is fine-tuned on these examples to shift from raw text prediction to instruction-following behavior. This phase uses far less data than pre-training but has a large effect on how the model responds in practice.
Human-written demonstrationsInstruction followingTens of thousands of examples
5
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
Aligning Outputs with Human Preferences
Human raters compare pairs of model outputs and rank them from best to worst. These rankings train a separate reward model that scores new outputs. The main model is then further fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward model's score, pushing it toward responses humans rate as helpful, honest, and safe. RLHF is the key mechanism behind the conversational quality of ChatGPT and Claude.
Pairwise human rankingsReward modelPPO optimizationSafety alignment
6
Evaluation & Safety Testing
Red-Teaming and Benchmarking
The model is evaluated on standardized benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, HellaSwag) and subjected to adversarial red-teaming to find failure modes. Safety teams probe for harmful outputs, bias, and jailbreak vulnerabilities. Results feed back into additional fine-tuning or data curation before public release.
MMLUHumanEvalRed-teamingBias evaluationSafety filtering
7
Deployment & Inference Infrastructure
Serving the Model at Scale
The trained model is deployed across distributed inference clusters. Techniques like model quantization (reducing numerical precision), distillation (training smaller models to mimic larger ones), and caching reduce inference costs. APIs abstract the model behind rate-limited endpoints. Ongoing monitoring tracks output quality, misuse patterns, and drift over time.
QuantizationDistillationAPI infrastructureMonitoringRate limiting

Resource Costs The four major categories
🗄️
Data
Acquisition & Curation
Collecting, cleaning, filtering, and storing the training corpus is a significant upfront cost. Web crawl data is technically free but requires massive infrastructure to process. Licensed content from publishers and data providers adds direct cost. Human annotation for SFT and RLHF is expensive per example.
Scale: GPT-3 trained on ~500B tokens. GPT-4 estimated at 1–2T tokens. LLaMA-3 trained on 15T tokens.
Annotation cost: ~$1–$20 per high-quality labeled example.
⚡
Compute
GPU / TPU Infrastructure
Pre-training requires thousands of high-end GPUs (A100, H100) or Google TPUs running in parallel for weeks to months. Hardware costs are either capital expenditure (buying) or cloud spend (renting). A single H100 GPU costs ~$30,000 to purchase or ~$2–$4 per hour to rent from cloud providers.
GPT-4 estimate: ~25,000 A100 GPUs for 90–100 days.
Estimated cloud cost: $50M–$100M+ for a frontier model pre-training run.
🔋
Energy
Power Consumption
Training large models consumes enormous amounts of electricity. A single A100 GPU draws ~400W under full load. A cluster of 10,000 GPUs running continuously consumes power equivalent to thousands of homes. Data center cooling adds another 30–50% on top of compute power draw.
GPT-3 training: estimated ~1,287 MWh of electricity.
GPT-4: estimated 50–100x more. Equivalent CO₂: hundreds to thousands of tonnes.
⏱️
Time
Training Duration
Even with thousands of GPUs running in parallel, pre-training large models takes weeks to months of continuous computation. Bugs, hardware failures, and loss spikes can require restarting long runs, multiplying time costs. Fine-tuning and RLHF add further weeks after pre-training completes.
Pre-training: 4–12 weeks typical for frontier models.
Full pipeline: 3–6 months from data finalization to public release.

💡
Key Insights What the data tells us
Cost doubles roughly every 18 months. Frontier model training costs have followed a steep upward curve, with each generation costing significantly more than the last. This concentrates frontier AI development among a small number of well-capitalized organizations.
Data quality matters more than data quantity. LLaMA 3 trained on 15T tokens outperforms models trained on fewer but lower-quality tokens. Curating and filtering training data is as important as scaling it.
RLHF is the differentiator for usability. The gap between a raw pre-trained model and a useful assistant is almost entirely explained by fine-tuning and RLHF. A model without alignment training is technically capable but practically unreliable.
Energy and hardware are structural bottlenecks. Access to thousands of H100 GPUs and the electricity to run them creates a hard barrier to entry. Cloud providers and national compute initiatives are the primary mechanisms enabling organizations without owned hardware to train at scale.
Open-weight models change the landscape. Meta's LLaMA series made frontier-class weights publicly available, enabling academic researchers and smaller organizations to fine-tune powerful base models without a full pre-training budget.
Inference costs accumulate beyond training costs. Serving a frontier model to millions of users generates ongoing GPU costs that can exceed the one-time training cost within months. Efficient inference architecture is as strategically important as training efficiency.
